# Service replay methods — working section for the revised manuscript

Status: supported method and pilot description; not a replacement for the submitted Methods or a statement that the complete manuscript is ready.

We retained the chronological order and resource requirements of production GPU jobs instead of resampling arrivals and repairing infeasible deadlines. The pilot cohort comprised completed GPU jobs submitted and completed within the first full Monday-start week of the published capacity calendar, 6–13 April 2020. The four cluster cohorts were fixed before comparing service allowances. All other allocations, including unsuccessful jobs and jobs crossing the cohort boundary, remained at their recorded times. Every arm used the same 192-hour domain, including the next day's observed background arrivals.

For job j, the work proxy W_j equals its recorded GPU count g_j multiplied by its recorded runtime. The release is its submission timestamp and the completion benchmark is its historical end time plus an ex ante allowance of 0, 6 or 24 hours. These benchmarks are retrospective comparisons, not contractual deadlines obtained from the trace. Neither the work proxy nor a counterfactual assignment of a common power–throughput curve establishes the job's useful training progress or measured energy consumption.

Two scheduling representations were evaluated. A fractional relaxation assigns GPU-hours x_jtm to job j and mode m in an event interval t. It enforces sum_tm q_m x_jtm = W_j, sum_m x_jtm <= g_j Delta_t, and sum_jm x_jtm <= (C_t − B_t) Delta_t, with no assignment outside the fixed job window. Events include job boundaries, background allocation changes and capacity changes. This relaxation does not certify fixed-size gang packing. Jobs that must run at their maximum rate throughout their entire window can be eliminated algebraically without changing the feasible region.

A separate constructive schedule executes each job once, without preemption, using its original g_j GPUs simultaneously. Unprocessed jobs retain their observed reservations. For each job, its reservation is released and replaced by a continuous interval that satisfies its release, completion benchmark and the remaining aggregate GPU capacity. The original reservation remains a fallback. An independent scan of all chosen intervals together with immutable background intervals verifies capacity, completed work and service windows against the raw records. GPU locality, device heterogeneity, model quality and mode-dependent overhead require additional validation.

We bracketed energy opportunities using this feasible construction and a separable per-job lower bound on energy that removes competition for cluster capacity. The bound is the lower convex envelope of the mode energy–throughput points including idle, subject to each job's work and maximum parallelism. An independent enumeration of pairs of modes verifies the bound. The resulting brackets describe model attainability, not statistical confidence intervals. Cases limited by solver time or matrix size are retained as unresolved; they are not treated as infeasible or removed from the experimental population.

The pilot assigns the same measured GPU curve to every eligible job and assumes a GPU idle ratio of 0.10. Occupied GPUs in the observed reference and fixed background are assigned full-reference GPU power; allocation records do not establish that this was their actual power draw. We report both cohort energy above this idle baseline and total modeled GPU energy including fixed background and idle capacity. No non-GPU component, whole-node energy or grid-level benefit is inferred from these pilot metrics. General conclusions require additional time windows, workload assignments, hardware calibration and integration with the power-system model.
